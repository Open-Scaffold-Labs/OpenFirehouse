# Changelog

All notable changes to OpenFirehouse are documented here.

The format follows [Keep a Changelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/).
Version numbers follow [Semantic Versioning](https://semver.org/) once
tagged releases begin; until then, this changelog tracks notable changes
on `main`.

## Unreleased

### Removed
- **The ISO Grading Report has been taken out. The numbers on it were not ISO numbers, and it had not loaded on production since June anyway.** Two separate problems, and the second one hid the first. The page asked the database for a column on pre-plan records that has never existed under that name, so every attempt to open it since 12 June returned an error panel — it was one of the seven queries the previous release flagged as "written against a data shape that doesn't exist, a redesign rather than a correction." This is that redesign. What the error panel was covering up matters more. **The score was not a Public Protection Classification score and could not be made into one by fixing the query.** Its four section weights were genuine — emergency communications 10, fire department 50, water supply 40, community risk reduction 5.5 — but almost nothing underneath them was. The measures were invented, as were the targets they were graded against: an average of 40 training hours per member, 24 training sessions, 12 drills, a maintenance record per apparatus per month. ISO grades none of those things. The entire 10-point emergency communications section was a single line reading "CAD integration active: true" — the word `true` typed into the source code, reading nothing, awarding full marks to every department forever. The certification measure counted a field that does not exist on training records, so it always came out at zero, and because zero was also its target it scored a **perfect** mark for measuring nothing. Between those two, about 16% of the score was awarded by things that did not look at any data. And the final figure was divided by 105.5 and multiplied by 100 to make a percentage, which is not how a PPC grade is read: an ISO report published this year shows a real department earning 83.03 of 105.5 points and being graded **Class 2**; as a percentage that is 78.7%, which is **Class 3**. The page would have told that chief they were a class worse than they are. A number that carries the units of an insurance rating, on a screen a chief takes into a field survey, has to be the real calculation or it has to not be there.
- **The estimated-PPC-credit columns on the training screens are gone too, for the same reason.** "Max PPC Credit" and "Est. Credit" took a member's percentage toward an hours target and multiplied it by the points available for that category. That is not ISO's formula — Item 580 is scored across the **whole department's** compliance, not one member's, and the arithmetic used was invented. The mapping underneath was wrong as well (below). The hours tracking itself stays and is the useful part: how many hours you have, against the requirement ISO actually states.

### Changed
- **The ISO training categories now match the real Fire Suppression Rating Schedule.** The table the app used had six categories. The schedule has eight, and five of the six were carrying the wrong point value, the section letters were shifted by one from C onward, and the single largest sub-item — the 35 points for use of training facilities — was missing entirely. The values summed to 97 rather than 100, which is the tell. Every entry has been rewritten against the primary document: a Public Protection Classification Summary Report prepared by ISO, effective 1 January 2026. The eight now read as the schedule reads them — training facilities and their use (18 hours a year), company training at the station (16 hours a **month**), classes for officers, new driver/operator (60 hours) and existing driver/operator (12 hours) as the two separate items they have always been, hazardous materials, recruit training, and pre-fire planning inspections. Each one carries ISO's own wording of its requirement so it can be checked rather than trusted. The arithmetic was verified by reproducing that report's own published result for this item to the cent. One correction worth naming: pre-fire planning inspections had been given a made-up "8 hours a year" requirement — **ISO does not state an hours figure for it at all.** It asks that every commercial, industrial and institutional building be inspected annually with up-to-date notes and sketches. That is a coverage requirement, not an hours one, so it no longer appears as an hours bar with a number nobody chose.
- **Wording on the training screens no longer claims things the app does not do.** Copy stating that hours "count toward your department's annual ISO audit" and "feed into your ISO PPC credit tracking" has been replaced with what is actually true: hours are grouped by schedule sub-item and shown against the requirement stated for each, and earned credit is scored by ISO at the department level and is not calculated here. The printed course certificate no longer stamps a section number next to the category, because a section number on a certificate reads as a credit claim.

### Added
- **Permit fees, invoices and receipts finally have screens. Until now every part of this was machinery with no way in.** The three previous releases built the billing engine and each said plainly that there was no screen and a clerk could not use it. This is the clerk. A new **Billing** tab in the Prevention Center holds four things. **Invoices** — the register a bureau works from, showing what was charged, what has been paid and what is still outstanding side by side, because one number cannot answer all three. Open one and you see its lines, every payment recorded against it, any correction linked to it, and which overdue notices have gone out. An issued invoice can be voided or corrected but never edited, so the register also filters to *just the voided ones* and *just the zero-dollar ones* in one click — those are the two piles an auditor asks for before looking at anything else. **Receipts** — recording money already received, and authorising refunds, each with its own permanent number. **Fee schedules** — writing a rate table, and formally adopting it, which requires naming the ordinance or resolution that adopted it, the body that passed it and the date; once adopted the rates are frozen and the only way to change one is to clone the version and adopt a new one. There is a dry-run calculator that works against a draft, so a bureau can check a rate table against the ordinance *before* it is in force. **Integrity** — the invoice and receipt number reports, and the one-file export of every charge, payment and refund for the finance office. And when a fee is committed, the screen shows the full working — which tier matched, which rate applied, what each step did to the number — because a person being asked to approve a figure they cannot check is not an approval. Most products in this market show a total and nothing else; this is a deliberate departure and it is written down as one.
- **Departments can now set their own invoice numbering and waiver policy.** Three settings, on the Prevention Center's Settings page, chief-only: the department code that goes in the middle of every invoice and receipt number, which month the fiscal year starts, and the dollar amount above which a refund or a fee waiver needs a second named approver. **That last one ships blank on purpose and we do not fill it in for you** — the threshold above which two signatures are required is a municipality's own policy, and a number nobody chose is still a number that governs money.
- **The database now checks our own queries before anyone can ship them.** The empty-calendar and blank-dashboard problems below shared one cause: a query can name a column that doesn't exist and nothing anywhere will object until a real person opens the screen and sees nothing. It isn't a spelling mistake a compiler can catch, the app builds cleanly around it, and no test touched it. Every fixed query in the product is now handed to the database itself to check — it parses and validates the query without running it — so a query naming something that isn't there is caught the moment it's written rather than by a department noticing a blank screen weeks later. This deliberately uses the database's own parser rather than a tool of our own that pattern-matches the code: an earlier attempt at exactly that reported 38 problems of which most were not problems at all, and a checker that can't be trusted is worse than none, because people start quoting its silence as proof. It reports rather than blocks for now, and the count it reports only ever gets ratcheted down.

### Added
- **The incident report now has a field for the time the 911 call came in — it never did.** A NERIS report requires three times from the 911 centre: when the call was created, when it was answered, and when it reached the centre. The form offered inputs for the second and third. For the first there was no box anywhere in the product, so the software filled it in from the incident's own clock — a value typed for a different purpose entirely. It is now the third input in the "911-center call times" section, alongside its two siblings, each with a line saying plainly what it means. As before, these come from your CAD feed automatically when you have one; you only type them if your dispatch centre gives you no data connection. And if nobody supplies it, the report says so and refuses to file rather than inventing a time to put on a legal record.
- **Every time field in the app now opens its picker when you tap anywhere in the box.** A time input only responded to the small clock icon at the right-hand edge — a target a few pixels wide, which on a mounted tablet at arm's length is effectively invisible, so the rest of the field read as dead. All nineteen of them, across incident reports, NFIRS reports, mutual aid, events, staffing and outreach, now open the picker from a tap anywhere in the field. Typing the digits still works exactly as before — this adds a way in, it doesn't replace one.

### Fixed
- **The moment a dispatcher clicked "Activate Incident" on the Command Board could end up recorded as the time the 911 call came in.** The Command Board is a live scene-management tool — a whiteboard for the incident commander. It was also, quietly, writing the incident record: closing a board offered "Save to Incident Log", and that saved report took its time from the moment the board was activated. On a call activated by hand rather than by a CAD dispatch, that is simply the moment somebody pressed the button — which then travelled onward and became the *call received* time on the federal NERIS report. The incident report is a legal document that can be subpoenaed, and the time a call came in is not something a piece of software should ever infer from a button press. **The board no longer writes the incident record at all** — not the report, not the link between a call and its report, not any timestamp. The times on a report now come from the CAD system that sent the dispatch, and if the CAD never sent one, the report says so plainly and refuses to file rather than filling the gap with a guess. Incidents are written where they were always meant to be written: by an officer, in the Incident Log, matched to the call by its CAD run number. Nothing else about the board changes — it keeps its own state, its accountability (PAR) clock and its MAYDAY record, which are its own tools and are not part of the legal record.

- **The Prevention Center's Settings › Department tab had been white-screening for weeks.** Opening it showed the loading spinner and then the generic "Something went wrong" panel — every time, for everyone, on production. The cause is a React rule: a component has to run the same list of internal state hooks on every render, and one of them had been written *below* the "still loading, show a spinner" early exit. So the first pass ran four and the pass after the settings arrived ran five, and React stops the component dead. It only breaks once the data lands, which is why it looked like a slow page rather than a broken one. This is also where the new billing settings live, so they were unreachable until this was fixed. Found by opening the page on the live site — every automated check we have was green and none of them *can* catch this: the build doesn't run the page, the server tests don't render anything, and our existing lint only looks for undefined names. There is now a dedicated check for this specific class of fault (`lint:hooks`), tested by deliberately re-introducing the bug and confirming it gets caught.
- **Nothing could be invoiced at all, and the reason was a setting with no way to set it.** Every invoice and receipt number contains a short department code. That code had no screen, no form and no API — it could only be put in place by writing to the database directly. Without it the system correctly refuses to issue anything, rather than stamping a permanent document with a hole where the department should be. So the entire invoicing and payments feature, built over three releases, was unreachable for anyone who did not have database access. Worse, the settings page would accept the field, report that it had saved, and change nothing — an unknown setting was quietly ignored instead of refused, which is precisely how this went unnoticed. Unknown settings are now rejected outright, loudly.
- **Approving a fee for exactly the amount the system calculated was being recorded as if you had overruled it.** The rule is that committing a *different* amount from the calculated one requires a written reason — a sensible control, and the published audits behind it found errors in both directions. But the comparison was made on the typed text rather than the value, so a calculated fee of $350.00 approved by typing "350" counted as a change: the clerk was refused, told to justify an override they had not made, and then — once they complied — the permanent audit record was stamped **overridden, with their reason attached**. A false entry, in the one part of the system whose entire purpose is being trustworthy under audit. Amounts are now compared as amounts. Approving a different figure still requires a reason, down to the cent.
- **When a fee or payment was rejected, the explanation arrived as raw computer output.** A mistyped amount produced a line of `{"code":"BAD_AMOUNT","at":"line 1",...}` on screen instead of "line 1 — amount is not a valid decimal". The fee screens had always done this properly; invoices and payments were written later and did not inherit it. All three now share one piece of code, so it cannot drift apart again.
- **A name typed into an invoice could become a live formula in the finance office's spreadsheet.** The export handed to a municipality's finance system quoted commas and quotation marks correctly but not a leading `=` or `+`, so a bill-to name beginning with one would be executed as a formula the moment the file was opened. It is now neutralised on the way out.

- **If one of OpenFirehouse's nightly jobs quietly stops running, the app now notices.** Five things run on a schedule behind the scenes — licence reconciliation, the radio-log cleanup, the NERIS submission sweep, scheduled report delivery, and the permit expiry ladder that moves permits from Active to Expired. Every way one of those can stop is *silent*: the key that lets the scheduler in gets rotated and the job is turned away before any of our code runs; a line in a config file gets dropped in a merge; the job starts and times out every night without finishing. None of those raises an error, so nothing could ever have emailed anyone about them — and a permit register that has quietly stopped ageing keeps showing lapsed permits as current, which a clerk reads as fact. Each job now records that it ran, and a **Scheduled jobs** panel on the Database Admin page shows, for each one, when it last completed and whether that's longer ago than its schedule allows. A job that has never run yet is said so plainly rather than being called overdue — on the day this shipped that was true of all five, and five red alarms on day one is how a warning light gets ignored. Detecting a job that has *stopped firing* is something we chose to build: it is not standard in this market, and the panel says so rather than implying otherwise.

- **A dispatch your CAD sends that we can't read is now something you get told about, on a screen, with a sound.** Since last week OpenFirehouse keeps every message a CAD sends, exactly as it arrived, even when the message is malformed and none of it can be understood — because when that happens the CAD does not try again and has no idea anything went wrong. Keeping it was half the job. The other half is that somebody has to find out, and until now those messages sat in a table nobody opens. There is now an **Interface faults** panel on the CAD Integration screen listing every message that couldn't be read — unreviewed ones first — showing which vendor sent it, when, from where, and why it failed. Officers and dispatchers can open the message itself and work the call by hand, which is the entire point of having kept it. Each one gets marked reviewed by a named person, with an optional note about what was done; that record is permanent and additive, so a later review never erases an earlier one. If several arrive inside an hour the panel says so plainly, because a run like that almost always means the vendor changed their message format and the fix is a phone call. And whoever is signed in doesn't have to be on that screen: an amber bar appears at the top of the app with a low, slow tone that is deliberately nothing like the dispatch tone — a fire-alarm panel distinguishes a *trouble* signal from an *alarm*, and so does this. **There is no dismiss button and no snooze.** A CAD sending unreadable messages is a configuration fault to fix with that vendor, and the bar's job is to make that conversation happen. It clears when the fault is reviewed.
- **You can now tell when a call number came from your CAD and when OpenFirehouse made it up.** Some CAD systems don't send a call number at all, so we assign one — otherwise there's nothing to file the call under. That has always been recorded and never shown, which meant a dispatcher reading a number off the screen had no way to know it wouldn't match the number being read out on the radio. Those now say so on the alert.
- **Payments on permit invoices — the recording half. There is still no screen for any of this; it is the machinery underneath, and a clerk cannot use it today.** Saying that first, same as the invoice entry did. A department can now record money it has received against an invoice — at the counter, in the mail, or through the city's payment processor — and every receipt is a pre-numbered, permanent record: `FY2026-AFD-R000001`, one unbroken run per department per year, with the same gap report an auditor asks for first, built to be permanently empty. **A receipt can never be edited.** Wrong amount, wrong invoice, wrong name — it gets voided, with a reason and a named approver, and the original stays readable forever with its number spent; then the right one gets recorded. Each receipt records what the public-money rules require: who paid, how much, **how** (cash, check with its number, card, ACH, money order), who took it, and when. **What an invoice still owes is never stored anywhere — it is worked out fresh from the ledger every time anyone asks**, so there is no second copy of the number to drift out of agreement. Partial payments just accumulate; paying too much shows as a negative balance rather than an error, because overpayment is a real thing that happens at a real counter. **Refunds are recorded, never paid out** — the record says a named person approved giving money back and why; the actual disbursement belongs to the municipality's finance office, as it does everywhere. Departments can also now set their own dollar threshold above which a refund or a fee waiver needs a **second named approver** — it ships unset, because that number is each municipality's own policy, not something software should invent. **Recording a payment finally gives the permit-issuance rule its teeth:** a permit whose fee is unpaid — or was assessed and never even billed — now refuses to issue, and says exactly which invoice and how much is outstanding. And the whole money trail — every charge, payment and refund — exports in one file for the finance office, which is the one thing every municipal purchasing document demands. Still deliberately absent, because no product in this market ships them: statements, write-offs, collection aging, automatic late-fee escalation, payment holds, and taking the card ourselves.
- **Two money rules that existed on paper now exist in the database (2026-08-04).** A quirk of how databases treat "unknown" meant a handful of our own completeness rules — a void must carry its written reason, a correction must name its approver — could be slipped past by leaving the field empty rather than filling it in wrongly. No screen ever did this and no record was affected; it was found by this release's own adversarial testing, on a rule written the same way in last week's invoice work. Both are now airtight, here and there.

### Fixed
- **The department calendar was empty for everyone, always.** Opening the calendar returned a blank month no matter what was scheduled — drills, meetings, shifts, hydrant tests, inspections, leave, everything. Not an error message, not a spinner: a clean, confident, empty calendar, which reads as "nothing is on" rather than "we couldn't load this." The calendar is assembled from 22 separate sources, and they all share one database conversation. The first source asked for a column by the wrong name; that ends the conversation, so all 21 behind it were refused too, and each one quietly returned nothing. One misspelling emptied the entire calendar. Each source is now isolated, so a source that fails takes only itself down and the other 21 still appear, and the system now records *which* source failed instead of silently substituting emptiness. A department could have been running on a calendar that showed nothing while everything was scheduled normally underneath.
- **The dashboard's Training badge showed a green 100% for departments that had entered no certifications at all.** The health scorecard scored an empty set as a perfect one. If nothing could be read — no certifications on file, or a fault reading them — the arithmetic came out at 100% and the badge went green, so a screen that meant *"we could not read anything"* told an officer *"you are fully compliant."* For a fire department that is the worst possible direction for a failure to point. Budget had the same bug pointing the other way and showed an alarming red 0% on departments that had simply never entered a budget. Both now say **"—" with "No cert data" / "No budget set"** in neutral grey. No data is not a passing grade and it is not a warning; it is the absence of an assessment, and it now says so.
- **Screens across the app were reading data by the wrong names and quietly showing nothing.** Twenty-three separate queries — behind the calendar, the dashboard, the ISO report, the assistant and several others — asked the database for columns and tables that had been renamed or had never existed. None of these produced a visible error; they produced empty lists, zeroes and blank panels, which look exactly like "there is nothing here." Every one was verified against the live database before being changed, and where the right answer genuinely wasn't clear, the query was left alone and flagged rather than guessed at — a wrong answer that loads is worse than a blank that doesn't. Seven remain open for that reason and are being worked deliberately; they are queries written against a data shape that doesn't exist, which is a redesign rather than a correction.
- **One broken Stripe key was making the whole nightly licence check look broken.** OpenFirehouse reconciles licences against Stripe in both live and test mode every night — a deliberate safety net that catches any paid invoice that didn't turn into a licence. Both ran in a single block, so when the test-mode key stopped working, the check gave up at that point: the live-mode result it had *already finished* was thrown away, the whole job reported a failure, and the message named only the test key — so there was no way to tell which half was broken, or that the other half had worked fine. Each mode now runs independently, both results are reported whatever happens to the other, and a failure says which mode and why ("test mode: Expired API Key") instead of a bare error code. Nothing is lost when a mode fails: each run re-checks the last three days of paid invoices against the licence ledger, so the next good run picks up anything missed. Found by the scheduled-jobs check on its first day in service.
- **Opening the CAD screen could switch off live dispatch alerts for the rest of your session.** Introduced and fixed the same day; it reached production for a few hours. The interface-faults panel and the trouble bar were listening on the same internal channel the app uses for live dispatch pushes, and when either one closed it shut that channel down for everything — so new calls stopped arriving instantly and fell back to the 20-second refresh, with nothing on screen saying so. Merely switching the panel between "Needs review" and "All faults" was enough to trigger it. They now use their own separate channel, and the shared machinery underneath counts its users so the last one out closes the door rather than the first. Found by an independent audit of the previous day's work, not by our own tests — the tests that would have caught it now exist.
- **The scheduled-jobs check could not see the failure it was built for.** A job never invoked even once — a schedule accidentally deleted, say — showed a grey "Not yet seen" indefinitely and counted as nothing needing attention, which is exactly the situation the check exists to catch. It now stops giving a job the benefit of the doubt once enough time has passed to rule out a fresh install. A job that runs and fails every single time is called out as *Failing* rather than lumped in with "hasn't run yet", because the two need different fixes. Also: a job that finished without doing anything at all was recorded as a success, and the real reason a job failed was being replaced on screen with "handler answered 500".
- **A database password could reach a log a chief can read.** If a nightly job failed to reach the database, the connection string — which contains a password — was written verbatim into the job history. One of the two places it could arrive was scrubbed and the other was not. Both are now.

### Security
- **Only a chief can add, change or delete a CAD connection.** Any signed-in member could do all three. That matters more than it sounds: adding a connection hands back a live webhook secret, and since last week that secret is the *only* credential the dispatch intake accepts — so it was, in effect, the ability to feed made-up dispatches into your own department. Deleting one silently cuts the link that carries your real ones. Reading the list stays open to everyone, deliberately: a crew that can't see whether their CAD link is up can't tell anyone it's down. Nobody loses a capability they were using — this is the same "one door locked, the other left open" fault we found on the riding board, this time on the other end of the same secret.

### Added
- **The incident report now opens in its own window.** Clicking Log Incident opens a separate browser window you can drag to a second monitor — the call list stays live and usable on the first screen instead of being greyed out behind a box. The old report was a popup fixed at 672 pixels wide: on a 1440 screen that was 47% of your display, and on a 1920 screen it *shrank* to 35%, because the width was capped and never grew. It showed **17 of the form's 110 fields** and took four and a half screens of scrolling to reach the Save button. The report window now fills the screen, lays the sections out in two or three columns depending on your monitor, and puts Save and Cancel in a bar that's always visible at the bottom. Measured on a 1440 screen: **17 fields visible became 37**, and 4.5 screens of scrolling became 2.3. On a 1920 screen: 19 became 43, and 3.8 screens became 1.2. If your browser blocks popups, the old in-page form still opens instead — the button always does something.
- **Your typing is kept if the window closes.** A report you haven't saved yet is now remembered on that computer, so closing the window, closing the tab, or restarting the browser doesn't lose it. It's deliberately **local only** — a half-written report is not visible to anyone else in the department until you actually save it, which is the right default for a record that can be subpoenaed. It says "Draft saved on this computer" rather than just "Saved", because it isn't in the system yet and shouldn't read as though it is. It is not a backup: clearing your browser data loses it, and it doesn't follow you to another machine.

### Fixed
- **A floating button was sitting on top of "Delete incident".** The three round buttons pinned to the bottom-right of every screen claim the outer 68 pixels of the window, and the Incident Log's row actions ran into that band — a hit-test measured the Delete control up to **68% covered**, on four rows at once. Delete is irreversible and it's on a legal record, so it was both hard to click and easy to mis-aim. The "Analyze" buttons on the AI Intelligence page had the same problem to a lesser degree. Both screens now reserve that strip. Worth stating plainly: the obvious fix — adding space at the bottom of the page — cannot work, because those buttons are pinned to the *window*, not the page, so they sit over some row at some scroll position no matter how much room the document has.
- **The report form now behaves like a window for people who don't use a mouse.** Opening "Log Incident" put a form over the page but never told screen-reader software a dialog had opened, and never moved the keyboard into it — pressing Tab walked the incident list *behind* the form. It now announces itself by name, puts your cursor on the first control, and keeps Tab inside until you close it. **Escape deliberately does not close it:** an officer half-way through typing a narrative should not lose it to a stray keypress, and no accessibility rule asks for that — the Close button is reachable from the keyboard, which is what's actually required. This is shared plumbing, so the rest of the app's windows can be brought up to the same standard one screen at a time.
- **Emoji replaced with real icons on the incident and NERIS screens** — 🔥 🏥 🛡 ⚠ in section headings and risk alerts. They draw as a different picture on Windows, Android and macOS, don't match the weight of the text beside them, and get read aloud mid-sentence by a screen reader. One place could not be fixed the same way and was handled differently: the incident Category dropdown, because a dropdown option can only contain plain text — the emoji there was simply removed, since the option already reads "Fire", "Medical" or "Hazmat".
- **The smallest text on the incident screens was 8 pixels.** Day-of-week and hour labels on the analytics charts, and roughly thirty other labels at 10px, are now 11px. They already met the readability standard after the earlier colour fixes; this is about a chief actually being able to read them.
- **Two more pieces of unreadable text, both only visible while a screen is still loading** — "Loading units…" on the dispatch board and "Loading your leave…" on the roster. They appear for a second or two on a slow connection and had never been captured by any check. The second is the mirror of the problem fixed earlier this week: the same grey that was too light on a white background is too *dark* on the black one.
- **A new incident report opened dated for TOMORROW if you started it after about 8pm.** The date field on a new report was filled in using Greenwich time rather than yours, so from roughly 20:00 an officer writing up a call was handed a form pre-dated to the next day — on a record that can be subpoenaed. This is the third place the same fault has turned up (The Board's calendar, then the Duty Schedule's, now here); each had its own private copy of the code, which is why fixing the first two didn't fix this one. The After-Action and Incident Cost forms had it too and are fixed in the same change.
- **And the clock on that form was frozen at whenever the page had been loaded.** The time field was filled in from a value worked out once, when the app first started up — not when you opened the form. On a watch-desk browser left open all tour, a report opened at 18:00 offered you the time the page had been opened that morning. Measured on the live site before the fix: the form offered 19:33 while the clock read 19:34, one minute after loading.
- **After-action reports were showing you a raw computer timestamp.** The date on each report in the list read `2026-03-21T00:00:00.000Z`. It now reads `03/21/2026`. Worth noting because the obvious fix is a worse bug — the naive one moves every report back a day for anyone west of Greenwich — so the code that formats it is deliberately written to never do that, with tests that fail if someone later tries.
- **48 pieces of unreadable text on the incident screens, including 32 on the report form itself.** The AI Incident Intelligence page and the After Action page had never been checked at all. Worse, the Incident Log *had* been checked and passed — but only the collapsed list was ever looked at: the expanded call detail and the report form open on a click, so the screen where an officer actually writes the legal record had never once been measured. Stat labels, NERIS validation warnings, the completeness readout, attachment and document labels, weekday and hour axis labels were between 2.4:1 and 3.7:1 against a 4.5:1 floor. All fixed, both themes, and the report form and expanded call detail are now checked on every build so this can't come back quietly. Also caught: the "Ready" and "N required missing" badges on the completeness panel were unreadable against their own tinted pill — a colour can pass on a white card and fail on a coloured one, and the failing badge was on the branch nobody had ever rendered.
- **Some grey text got darker in dark mode instead of lighter.** Nine places on the incident and NERIS forms had their light and dark colours the wrong way round.
- **The Duty Schedule calendar was highlighting the wrong day every evening — a second time, in a second calendar.** Same fault as The Board's calendar: it asked for the date in Greenwich time rather than yours, so from about 8pm the red "today" marker jumped to tomorrow. It had its own private copy of the code, which is why fixing the first calendar didn't fix this one. Leave requests spanning several days could also paint the wrong squares for anyone east of Greenwich.
- **The Duty Schedule's summary row was colour-coded backwards.** "0 Understaffed Days" — which is good news — carried an amber warning triangle, while "25 No Coverage", the worst number on the page, sat in plain grey. The three cards were coloured by what they count rather than by what the count means. A zero problem count is now a green tick, and the number that needs attention is the one that looks like it.

### Added
- **Permit invoices — the record-keeping half. There is no screen for this yet, and there is no way to record a payment.** Saying that first because the rest of this entry describes real machinery, and it would be easy to read it as something a clerk can go and use today. They cannot. What exists is the ledger underneath: a department can now raise an invoice against a permit or an inspection, and what it can do to that invoice afterwards is deliberately, permanently limited. **An issued invoice can never be edited.** Not the amount, not the name on it, not the date — there is no "edit invoice" anywhere in the system, and there is no "reopen". If it's wrong you either **void** it or issue a linked **adjustment**, and in both cases the original stays exactly as it was, readable forever, with a reason, a named approver and who did it. Voiding is not deleting: the invoice keeps its number, keeps its amount, and stays in the register marked void — because the published guidance for public money says a voided receipt must be *retained*, not removed. Numbers run as `FY2026-AFD-000001`, one unbroken run per department per year, and the system will show you any number it handed out that has no invoice behind it — that report is the first thing an auditor asks for, and it is built to be permanently empty. A **$0 invoice is a real invoice** with a written reason, not a blank — a fee that quietly came out as nothing is the specific thing auditors find, so the software refuses to produce one by accident: if it can't work out what to charge, it says so and charges nothing at all rather than guessing zero. Every figure is worked out from the lines by the server, using exact whole-cent arithmetic rather than ordinary decimals, because ordinary decimals genuinely get this wrong — in one published review of 840 permits the errors ran to $11,127, in *both* directions. And the person who can look at an invoice is not automatically the person who can raise, void or write off one: inspectors can read the register, only prevention admins can change it. Chasing an unpaid invoice is recorded as dates — second notice sent, final notice sent, referred — each written down once, by a person, and never rewritten afterwards. **Nothing escalates on a timer**, and nothing files a lien: those are decisions with a hearing behind them, not something software should do while nobody is looking.
- **Colour contrast across the app is now checked automatically, and it can say "I could not check".** Text that's too faint to read against what's behind it is the easiest fault to ship and the hardest to notice, because it looks fine to whoever chose the colours. There's now an automatic check that reads every piece of text and every form box on a screen, works out the real contrast, and fails the build if anything falls below the accessibility standard — in both light and dark themes. Three things it deliberately gets right, each learned from getting it wrong: it checks the text *inside* boxes and dropdowns, not just ordinary text, which is exactly where the unreadable setup fields were hiding; it separates "this is definitely too faint" from "I can't tell, because this floats over whatever is behind it" from "this one is a switched-off button, which the standard exempts"; and for dropdown lists, whose appearance is controlled by the operating system rather than by us, it says outright that it cannot measure them instead of reporting them as fine. A check that can't see something now says so, rather than staying quiet and being mistaken for a pass.

### Added
- **Your department can now define its own fee schedule, and a permit fee is calculated from it instead of typed in. There is no screen for it yet.** Being straight about that first: this is reachable through the API only, so nothing new appears in the app today and a clerk cannot use it yet. What exists underneath is the part that has to be right before any screen is worth building. A fee schedule is **adopted**, not edited: it records the ordinance, exhibit, code appendix or board resolution that authorises it, who adopted it and on what date, and once adopted **the numbers are frozen** — changing a fee means adopting a new version, and the old one stays readable forever so a charge raised last year can still be explained. Published audits of fire-marshal offices keep finding the same two failures — fee tables that could be edited in place with no record of the change, and a schedule "in force" that traced back to a board action years earlier that nobody could produce. Neither is possible here. The calculation covers what real adopted fee schedules actually do rather than a simplified version of it: a flat fee per permit type; a table keyed on square footage, occupant load, sprinkler heads, licensed beds, tank count and a dozen other measures; a two-dimensional table (occupancy group crossed with floor area); hours at an hourly rate, with **both** an after-hours multiplier **and** a minimum-hours floor, because roughly half of the schedules we studied charge one way and half the other and a system that only does one cannot express the other; a percentage of another fee; and a technology or admin surcharge that applies to **only the lines it is meant to apply to**, since several jurisdictions explicitly exclude their hourly, re-inspection and appeal lines from it. Those pieces also chain together, which is how the real ones work — floor area produces a construction valuation, the valuation produces a building fee, and the fire fee is a percentage of that. Every calculation is stored with the figures it was given and a line-by-line breakdown, so a total can always be checked rather than taken on trust. **The arithmetic is exact.** Money here is never handled as an ordinary decimal number, because the usual kind cannot represent amounts like 1.005 and rounds them down; an audit of one permitting office found $11,127 of fee errors in *both* directions, which is exactly what that produces. **Nothing is ever quietly assumed to be zero.** If a measurement is missing, or a building falls outside every band in the table, the system refuses and says which one — it does not hand you a $0 fee. A genuine zero-fee permit, like a municipally owned building, is something a person records with a reason on it, and it stays findable as its own thing, because a fee-free permit is the first record any auditor asks to see. **The calculation proposes; a person decides.** A fee is computed automatically and then has to be committed by hand, and committing a different amount from the computed one requires a written reason — the reduction is allowed, an unexplained one is not. Once committed it cannot be quietly altered. Waivers likewise always carry a reason and the name of who approved them. And the people who do inspections **cannot change the fee table at all** — they can look it up and calculate with it, which they need, but authorising the rates and committing charges are separate jobs held by separate people, because holding all of it at once is the finding that appears in every one of these audits.
- **What the fee work deliberately does not do: take payments.** OpenFirehouse records what is owed. It does not process cards, hold money, or act as your merchant — that stays with your municipality's existing finance system and payment provider, which is also how the rest of this market works. It will not place a lien, and it will not block a permit over an unpaid balance.
- **The marketing site now carries the NERIS V1 Data Exchange Compatible badge.** FSRI issues that badge to RMS and CAD software that passes its API compatibility check, and OpenFirehouse passed it on 3 August 2026. The site used to say "NERIS-ready for the 2026 transition" — our own word for it. That is now replaced by the badge itself, which is somebody else's word for it. It is shown as a badge and nothing more: it means the software meets the technical standard for exchanging data with NERIS, and it does not mean NERIS endorses, recommends or certifies the product — which is exactly what the badge's own terms say it means, and the only thing we claim by it.
- **The Client ID you have to paste into the NERIS portal now has a Copy button.** Authorising OpenFirehouse to file for your department means pasting one long code — `3551da5a-ea50-47c1-8606-0687ea0d96e4` — into the NERIS portal's Enrollments page. - **A copy button that says "Copied!" has actually copied.** Browsers are allowed to refuse a page's request to write your clipboard, and they sometimes refuse silently — which means a button can flip to "Copied!" over a clipboard that never changed. That is worse than no button: you paste with total confidence and get whatever you copied twenty minutes ago. Every copy button on the settings screen now checks the result, uses a second, older copy method if the first is refused, and only says "Copied!" when a copy genuinely happened. ### Added

### Added

### Added
- **Every message your CAD sends us is now kept, word for word, before we try to read it.** Until now, if a dispatch arrived in a form we couldn't understand, it was gone — and worse, we had nothing to show for it. That is a bigger problem than it sounds, because of how these connections work: the national standard that governs them tells the sending system to do **nothing** when a message comes back rejected. There's no retry, no queue, nothing to go back and look for. The dispatch center believes the call was delivered. We simply never had it. From now on, the moment a message arrives and we've confirmed it came from your department, the raw text is written down and saved *before* anything tries to interpret it. If we can read it, the call goes to the board as always. If we can't, we still have every character of it, along with when it arrived and where it came from, so a person can read it and work the call by hand. Nothing is ever thrown away for being unreadable.
- **Two ways a dispatch could vanish silently, both now closed.** The first: if your CAD sent a message that was cut off or garbled partway through, our software rejected it before the part that does the saving ever ran — so the very messages most worth keeping were the ones guaranteed to be lost. The second: dispatch systems label what they send, and if the label didn't match what we expected, the message was accepted with a cheerful "OK" and then read as if it were **empty**. No error, no warning, nothing in a log. Some real dispatch vendors do label their messages this way. Both are fixed, and the second now works properly rather than merely failing loudly.
- **These records are kept permanently and cannot be altered.** The stored messages can be added to but never edited or deleted — not by the application, not by anyone using it. They are never purged on a schedule, because the record of what your dispatch center actually sent you is exactly the kind of thing that matters years later, in a lawsuit or an investigation, long after any retention timer would have erased it. Your dispatch credentials are deliberately stripped out before anything is stored, so a permanent record never becomes a permanent copy of your password.

### Security
- **Each department now has its own dispatch key, and the shared one is gone.** Previously the platform could accept a single deployment-wide key for incoming CAD messages, alongside each department's own. That's been removed entirely — a department's own key is now the only thing accepted. Three reasons. A shared key means rotating it for one department rotates it for every department at once. A shared key also doesn't identify *whose* dispatch a message is, which is precisely what we need to know before we can safely file it. And there was a configuration in which, if the platform were deployed a particular way, the shared key could end up unset and incoming messages accepted with no key at all — that possibility no longer exists in any configuration.
- **"Your key is wrong" and "we couldn't check your key" are no longer the same answer.** If our database is briefly unreachable, that is our problem, not your dispatch center's — and it's the one situation where we want them to send the call again. Previously any such hiccup was reported the same way as a bad key, which tells the sending system to give up permanently. A live call could have been discarded because of a momentary blip on our side. The two are now answered differently, so a temporary fault asks for a retry and only a genuinely bad key is refused.

### Added
- **Permits now understand that a term ending isn't the same as a permit being dead.** Until now a permit was either in force or expired, with nothing in between — which is not how a permit bureau actually works, and not how the systems fire departments buy work either. There are now four stages instead of two. A permit runs normally, then enters its **renewal window** while it is still perfectly valid — the holder is operating lawfully, and this is simply when renewing becomes possible. When the term date passes it becomes **lapsed but in grace**: still renewable, and this is the point late fees are measured from. Only past the end of grace does it become **expired**, and that is the door closing — at that point renewing is no longer offered and the holder starts a fresh application. Each department sets its own notice window and grace period; a department that wants no grace at all sets it to zero. The stages are set by the system on a schedule rather than by anyone remembering to do it.
- **What a permit says is fixed the moment it's issued.** A permit records the term, the notice window and the grace period that were in force on the day it was issued, and the database itself refuses to let those be changed afterward. So when your department later adjusts a permit type — a longer term, a different fee, a new grace period — the permits already in businesses' hands are untouched, and the change applies only to permits issued from then on. This matters because the alternative is silent: without it, editing a setting would quietly rewrite the terms of permits that were already issued, served, and relied upon, with nothing on screen to say it had happened.
- **A permit catalogue your department writes itself.** Permit types are now a list your department owns — each with its own term rules, its own numbering prefix, whether it can be renewed, whether it requires an inspection, and whether it's visible only to staff (the default) or to applicants. Types are **retired, never deleted**, so a permit issued years ago always keeps the definition it was issued under. Expiration rules are versioned with effective dates: adding a new version closes the previous one and keeps it on file, so the question "what did our catalogue say on the day we issued this?" always has an answer.
- **The catalogue and the expiry schedule are now screens, not just plumbing.** Prevention Center → Permits has three views: the **Register** you already had, **Permit types**, and **Expiry job**. Permit types is where a clerk writes the catalogue — add a type, point it at an expiration rule, retire one that's no longer used. Two things the screen tells you rather than making you find out: a type with no expiration rule is flagged in the list, because permits of that type can't be issued until it has one; and both the retire and new-version dialogs state plainly that **permits already issued are not affected** — they keep the terms they were issued under, and the database won't allow those to be altered.
- **You can see whether the nightly expiry pass is actually running.** The Expiry job view shows each night's run — what it checked, what it changed, and what it couldn't. It distinguishes *"hasn't run yet"* from *"behind"*, because on a newly set-up department the first is simply the truth and treating it as a problem would teach people to ignore the warning. If the pass hasn't completed in over 36 hours it says so, and says what that means for you: permits past their term keep reading as current until it runs again. It also shows how many permits **couldn't be checked** — permits issued before your catalogue existed carry no recorded term, so rather than guess at dates on a legal record the pass leaves them exactly as they are, and tells you the number instead of hiding it.
- **The register now shows the dates behind a permit's status**, because a colour can't carry them: a permit in its renewal window reads "renew by ⟨date⟩", and one past its term reads "term ended ⟨date⟩ · renewable N more days". Note what it deliberately doesn't say — nothing about whether a business may lawfully keep operating. That's your call as the authority having jurisdiction, not the software's.

If something needs to change, there are two honest paths instead of an edit. **Revoking** a permit requires you to pick the actual ground for it — the seven grounds the fire code lists, or a local ground with the local provision cited — plus a written explanation of what happened. Members can still do everything that's theirs to do: request leave, request a trade, respond to a vacancy or recall.
- **Overtime logs and shift trades work for multi-station departments.** Both were internally tied to a single-station assumption that would have rejected every OT entry and trade request in a department running more than one firehouse; both now key on the department itself (verified data migration, new automated tests). Closing a recall had the same flaw — it silently did nothing for multi-station departments — and is fixed.
- **Schedule dates can no longer drift by a day.** Generated shifts, FLSA work-period boundaries, and trade dates were computed through a UTC conversion that shifted them one day earlier for any US timezone; all schedule math now runs on plain calendar dates, locked to the rule that a shift belongs to the date it starts.
- **Timesheet generation now counts approved leave.** The leave lookup referenced fields that didn't exist and quietly failed — leave hours never appeared on any generated timesheet. It now counts the actual approved-leave days that overlap the pay period. FLSA settings are also read from the department (not a single station), so multi-station departments compute against the right work period.
- **Multi-station departments can no longer trip over a single-station assumption in daily-staffing data.** The Command Board, daily run list, apparatus seat assignments, and seat templates were all internally keyed on a legacy single-station identifier; in a department running more than one firehouse, that could have failed board saves or — in a narrow case — filed a record under the wrong department. All four now key directly on the department itself (matching how access control already isolates data), with a verified data migration and new automated tests covering the multi-station cases. No visible behavior changes for existing single-station departments.
- **Run-list import works again.** Importing a roster from PDF/CSV/Excel hit an internal error introduced with the seat-resolution upgrade and rolled itself back safely (no partial data was ever written); the importer now completes and reports exactly how many riders got seats. Imported members and rigs are also no longer tagged to a default station they don't belong to.
- **Header and sidebar text is easier to read.** Seven labels in the shared chrome (menu section names, header buttons, the role subtitle, the Live badge) sat below the readability standard for contrast; all now meet WCAG AA (4.5:1) — which also means they hold up better on a truck-mounted screen in sunlight.
- **A completed inspection now shows its result and violation statuses as read-only badges, not controls that look editable.** On a finalized record the result appears as one solid color-coded verdict (Pass / Fail / Reinspection Required) and each violation's status as a static badge, instead of dimmed pickers whose state was easy to misread. Recording service no longer bounces you off the Notice step back to Signatures, and the Mailroom's "all mailed" line no longer reads oddly above already-served notices in the Show-all view.
- **A notice can no longer be generated with placeholder text on it.** Until a department authors its own notice wording, generating a Notice of Violation is refused with a plain explanation of exactly which text blocks to fill in — previously the PDF quietly printed "[SAMPLE TEXT — review with your authority having jurisdiction]" in the body of a legal document.
- **Seven dead "coming soon" pages removed from the Inspections menu.** They were early mockups that duplicated features that now actually ship inside Prevention Center; old links to them land on Prevention Center instead of a blank screen.
- **A property's "open violations" count no longer double-counts.** Completed inspections are frozen records — their open violations are carried onto the scheduled reinspection, and the property card was counting both copies. The card now counts only what is currently open, matching the open-violations report.
- **Offline edits now sync themselves the moment the server is reachable again — and there's a sync button.** Previously, if the connection dropped and came back while the app stayed open on one screen, queued work sat undelivered until the app was closed and reopened. The app now quietly checks for the server while offline and delivers queued work as soon as it answers; the sync status chip in the header shows how many edits are waiting and can be tapped to sync on demand.

### Changed
- **A violation now carries one code — the real one.** The violation code library used to show two numbers on every row: an internal index the software invented (1001, 2002, …) next to the real fire-code section it stood for. An inspector cites one code, and a made-up number has no business on a legal notice. The invented index is gone: the code IS the adopted fire-code section (shown as, e.g., "IFC 2021 §906.1") in the code library, the cite screens on web and iPad, and the starter set — with each section deep-linked to the free official code text.
- **Call closing now matches the standard exactly: a CAD close event or a manual clear — nothing more.** Removed the opt-in auto-expiry timer that could close a call after N hours; a call is over only when the CAD says so or a person clears it. (A machine shouldn't decide an incident is over on a clock.)
- **Clear-time disposition is now the NERIS standard's own value set** (Cancelled / Staged-Standby / No Incident Found) instead of an internal list, so a cleared call's outcome flows straight into NERIS reporting with no re-translation.

### Fixed
- **NERIS details entered on an incident report were silently discarded on save.** The incident form collected the NERIS classification, the actions/tactics taken, and the hazmat detail block — and the server quietly dropped all of it, so a live incident's export carried a generic default type and no actions at all. Those fields now persist on the incident record, are checked against the national standard's own value sets exactly (a misspelled or outdated value is rejected with a plain explanation, never silently accepted or "close-enough" matched), and the database itself refuses a record that claims both "here's what we did" and "nothing was done."
- **The NERIS export no longer stamps local times as UTC or falls back to a hardcoded department ID.** A second, older copy of the export logic had survived on the server with both bugs the main path had already fixed — every timestamp shifted by the timezone offset, and a missing department ID silently became another department's. There is now exactly one export path; a missing department ID is reported as an error to fix, never invented; and an incident type that can't be mapped to the standard is surfaced for a human instead of being quietly exported as a generic service call.
- **A declared MAYDAY now actually saves — and a best-effort audit write can no longer sabotage the operation it was recording.** The audit trail is meant to be best-effort: its own failure must never fail the user's action. But under the per-request transaction model, a failed audit write poisoned the whole transaction, so the real work — a sealed MAYDAY record, in the case that surfaced this — would silently roll back even though the request reported success (a green "declared," a 201, nothing saved). Audit writes are now isolated with a database savepoint: a failed audit rolls back only itself and leaves the operation intact, so the "never fails the operation" guarantee is finally true across every audited write in the system, not just the one that exposed it.
- **The PAR check on the Command Board no longer starts everyone as "accounted for."** The check-in list opened with every firefighter pre-checked and green, so an officer who opened it and hit Submit without reading recorded a clean, all-accounted-for PAR for the entire fireground — without confirming a single person. A Personnel Accountability Report is the tool you reach for when you think you may have lost someone; its default answer cannot be "everyone is fine." Accounting for a firefighter is now an affirmative act: nobody is counted until they are checked in, and the Submit button says how many are still missing. (A one-tap "All ✓" is kept for a genuine fast all-clear — but it is now a deliberate choice, not the silent default.)
- **A PAR that fails to reach the server is no longer lost, and no longer asks the officer to re-run it.** If the network dropped when a PAR was recorded, the board used to show a message telling the officer to re-run it once they had a connection — which nobody does on a fireground — while the PAR itself went nowhere. Now a completed PAR is a durable record: it is stamped with the real time it happened, queued on the device if the network is down, and syncs itself the moment connectivity returns, without ever double-recording. The officer runs the roll call once; the software makes sure it lands.
- **Every certification entered through OpenFirehouse now counts toward staffing.** Certifications added through the app were stored under their display name ("Firefighter I") while the apparatus seat requirements were matched by code ("firefighter_1") — so the two never matched, and a fully qualified firefighter was scored as *unqualified* on the staffing board. Any cert a department had entered simply did not count. The two sides now speak the same language, on both staffing views, and a certification the app can't recognize is surfaced for review rather than silently treated as missing.
- **The PAR clock now always starts.** The countdown to the next PAR was measured from the first unit's on-scene time — but that time only exists if someone marks a unit on-scene, which does not happen on every call. On the calls where it was never marked, the clock silently never started. It now counts from the dispatch time, which is present on every call, so the PAR timer can never quietly fail to run. (Departments with long travel times can still choose on-scene anchoring; it is no longer the default, and the board always labels which one it is using so it agrees with what dispatch announces over the radio.)
- **The live command board can no longer be overwritten by just anyone.** The board's write endpoint required a login but not a role, so any member of the department — including a probationary firefighter, or an in-cab tablet — could overwrite the department's active command board. It is now restricted to dispatch and command, matching every other action on that board.
- **An inspection can no longer be recorded as passing while violations are still open — on any device.** The rule that a building cannot pass with unabated violations was real, but it was only enforced on one of the ways an inspection could be completed. The iPad wrote through a different door that skipped the check entirely, so a building could be recorded as passing with an open violation and no reinspection was ever scheduled — the violation simply fell out of the loop. That door is closed: completing an inspection now goes through one path, on every device, and that path always runs the check and always schedules the reinspection. The pass/fail result itself is now a fixed set of values the whole system agrees on, checked exactly rather than guessed from the wording — a subtle earlier version could be defeated by writing "Passed" instead of "Pass." A completed inspection is now genuinely final and read-only; correcting one is a formal amendment, not a quiet edit.
- **The pass/fail vocabulary is now defined in exactly one place and shared to every device.** The list of inspection results (Pass, Fail, Reinspection Required, Not Completed) used to be copied into the web app and the iPad app separately and kept aligned by hand — which is how the iPad once offered a result ("Conditional") that no other part of the system recognized and the server would reject. All three now read the same single, server-owned list, and an automated check fails the build if a copy ever drifts — so a device can never again present a result the record cannot accept.
- **The inspection app on the iPad no longer tells an officer a building passed when the server refused it.** When the server declined a change — for example, refusing to pass a building with an open violation — the iPad used to file that refusal away as "still saving," retry it forever, say nothing, and keep the rejected value on screen. An officer could walk away from a building believing it passed when the record said otherwise. Now a refusal is shown immediately and unmistakably, the rejected value stops being displayed, and the officer is told plainly what the server would not accept and why. A change that genuinely just needs signal still says so; a change the server *refused* now says *that* instead — the two are never confused again. And a completion that reached the server but whose reply got lost to a dead cell is recognized as already-done, not reported as a failure.
- **Inspections are an iPad surface, and now the app enforces that** — a phone can no longer reach the inspection screens even by a stray link. It was never a security gap (the server already refused a phone's writes), but it would have put an officer in front of controls whose every action was silently declined.
- **Building intel and hydrants now reach the rig even when the rig has no signal.** The size-up screen used to ask the server for the matched pre-plan and the nearby hydrants at the moment a call dropped — and if that request failed, it drew an empty card. Which meant that in a basement, or a rural dead zone, "no pre-plan on file for this address" and "we could not reach the server" looked **exactly the same** to the officer reading it. An absence of signal could be read as an absence of a building, or of a water supply, and a crew could make an entry or water-supply decision on it. The department's pre-plans and hydrants now live **on the device**, synced quietly in the background, and the address is matched on the device too — so nothing has to be fetched during a call at all. When there is genuinely no plan for an address, the app says so plainly. When it simply does not know, it says *that* instead, and tells the crew not to assume: *"Do not assume there is no water."* Every layer now carries its own age — when it last synced, when the plan was last updated, when the hydrant was last flow-tested — because a five-year-old flow rating and a fresh one should not look alike on a fireground. And a hydrant marked out of service is finally shown as out of service, rather than drawn like a working one.

### Added
- **The incident report now captures everything the national standard asks for — and only shows you what your incident needs.** Pick up to three incident types (one primary); the form reveals only the sections those types make relevant. Fire incidents get the full fire module (structure or outside branch — floor and room of origin, conditions on arrival, damage rating, cause, water supply, investigation); medical incidents get per-patient entries (care evaluation, transport, status); and every report can record structured mutual aid, and casualties and rescues person-by-person — including who performed a rescue and how the person was removed. A live completeness meter shows, as you type, exactly what the national system would accept, flag, or reject — computed by the same engine that builds the submission, so the meter can never disagree with the export.
- **Reports now have a review chain: draft → in review → approved.** An officer submits the report for review; an officer-or-above approves it or returns it to draft. Approval locks the report's NERIS content against edits (with an audited unlock, never a silent one), and every transition is recorded — who, when, and any notes. Live submission to the national system will hang off "approved."
- **911-center call times now come from your CAD feed automatically.** The dispatch webhook now recognizes call-answered and call-received timestamps in vendor payloads and carries them straight into the report — these were never a firefighter's to know or type. Departments whose dispatch center offers no data connection can still transcribe them from the dispatch report in a clearly-marked fallback section.
- **Incident reports now speak NERIS natively.** The incident form's classification, actions & tactics, and no-action reason are recorded in the national reporting standard's own plain-language vocabulary — generated directly from the live NERIS system (with an automated drift check so the app's choices can never silently fall out of date with the standard, on the form or on the server). Recording an outcome is now an explicit either/or: what was done, or why nothing was done — never both. Hazmat incidents gained the standard's two required outcome fields (disposition and people evacuated). The NERIS export now produces the live federal submission format, with a validation report alongside every export — what would be accepted, what's missing, and a completeness score — so a department can see exactly where a report stands before it ever goes to the national system.
- **The code library grew from a 26-code starter to a 79-section IFC 2021 master library.** One tap now loads the sections fire inspectors actually cite — egress, fire protection ITM, fire lanes/hydrants/FDC access, address identification, commercial cooking hoods and grease cleaning, space heaters and temporary wiring, firestopping, decorations, hot work permits, gas cylinder securing, LP-gas storage, vacant buildings, and existing-building smoke/CO alarms — every section verified against the official published text and reviewed by a working fire inspector, each deep-linked to the free official code text. Departments curate down from there: deactivate what the bureau doesn't cite, edit titles, or import their own adopted list. This is the vendor-maintained-library model the market's inspection platforms use, sized to stay usable in the field.
- **The starter violation-code library now cites the real fire code.** The 26 starter codes every new department can load now carry actual IFC 2021 section numbers (verified against the official published text, reviewed by a working fire inspector) instead of internal shorthand — so a notice built from a starter code cites, for example, IFC 2021 §1032.2 for a blocked exit. Each code also carries a deep-link: citing a violation on the web or on the iPad now shows a "View official text" link that opens that exact section in the free official code reading room, on demand and never bundled into the app. The section and edition appear right in the code picker on both devices, including on a never-synced iPad working offline from its built-in fallback list. Departments on a state or local code adjust the same way as before — edit any code, or import their own list.
- **The Command Board now has a MAYDAY button.** When a firefighter is missing, trapped, or down, command declares a MAYDAY with one guarded (two-tap) press. The whole board turns red, an elapsed clock starts, and a PAR is ordered automatically — the single most important accountability trigger on a fireground, which until now raised nothing. The declaration is sealed into an append-only, tamper-evident record: the database itself will not permit that record to be edited or deleted after the fact, which no command board on the market claims. Resolving a MAYDAY is gated on a whole-scene PAR, so a rescue cannot be quietly closed before everyone is accounted for. It is deliberately tap-and-snapshot rather than a form to fill in — the down firefighter's location and status stay on the radio, where they belong, because a chaotic scene at 0300 is no place to type. Every action is a single tap, server-timestamped and attributed, and it survives a page reload.
- **Reassign a reinspection to another inspector.** When the inspector who cited the original violations can't perform the follow-up (out sick, off, moved on), a chief or prevention admin can now hand the *pending* reinspection to someone else — right from the queue. Pick the new inspector, add an optional reason, and it moves into their queue. Every reassignment is written to the audit trail (who moved it, to whom, when, and why), and a completed inspection — a finalized legal record — can never be reassigned.
- **A Mailroom for the end of the day.** Every violation notice you generate now collects in one place — the Prevention Center's new **Mailroom** tab — newest first, so a clerk can see everything that still needs to go out at a glance. View any notice's PDF, mail it, and mark it sent (certified or first-class); marking a notice records a real service event on the inspection record, not just a checkbox. A one-tap filter shows only what still needs mailing, with a running count.
- **Import and export your violation-code library as a spreadsheet.** Building a department's citation list no longer means typing every code by hand. Upload a CSV of your adopted codes; the system validates it and shows a preview — how many codes will be added versus updated, plus any rows with problems and why — *before* anything is saved, so there are no surprises. Export your whole library at any time to back it up or hand it to another department, and download a ready-made template to fill in. Re-importing is safe: a blank cell never erases wording you already entered, and duplicate rows within a file are handled cleanly. Exported files are hardened against spreadsheet formula-injection. Import is limited to prevention administrators.
- **The command board can now receive a real arrival time from CAD.** OpenFirehouse already knew the instant a call was dispatched; it now also accepts unit *status* updates from a dispatch feed — en route, on scene, available — the same way the major command boards get them. When the dispatcher marks the first unit on-scene, that time flows in and starts the fireground clock, with the unit that arrived and the time it happened both recorded. It is never inferred from GPS or a geofence — it is the dispatcher's own action, relayed — and a status word the system doesn't recognize is skipped and reported, never guessed. (Whether a given department receives arrival depends on its CAD feed; a basic alerting relay only sends the initial dispatch. The board is now ready to use it the moment a richer feed is connected.)
- **The Command Board prompts for a PAR when the fireground changes, not just when a timer expires.** A PAR is owed at real turning points — an emergency evacuation, a switch from offensive to defensive, a sudden collapse or flashover, a primary-search all-clear, an incident brought under control — and a board that only nags on a wall clock misses them. Those events are now a distinct row on the board (kept separate from the routine milestone strip so an order to get everyone out is never dressed up as a normal step): declaring one is timestamped into the record and raises a **PAR REQUIRED** prompt immediately, regardless of the countdown. The board prompts; a human still runs the roll call over the radio — the software never records a PAR nobody performed.
- **Personnel on the board now ride a rig.** A firefighter could be shown "on scene" with no apparatus at all, which let the board read "0 units on scene · 3 personnel" — not a real state, because firefighters arrive on apparatus, in a seat. Each person on the board now carries the rig they rode in on, and the crew is shown grouped by apparatus, the way a passport accountability system works. The one real exception — a volunteer who arrives in their own vehicle — is not left floating: they land in a loud UNASSIGNED pool until Command gives them a job, so freelancing is impossible to hide. And if the board and the fireground ever disagree (someone shown on-scene from a rig that isn't), the board says so and asks Command to reconcile it over the radio rather than quietly rendering a number it can't stand behind.
- **The offline queue now syncs on more than just regaining signal.** Everything the app queues when a write fails — a PAR, an incident save — depended on a single trigger: the device going from offline back to online. But a write can fail while the device still has signal (a momentary server hiccup, a proxy error), and in that case nothing drained the queue until the next time the network actually dropped and came back, or the page was reloaded. The queue now also drains when the app is opened, when a new item is queued, when the officer returns to the tab, and on a short repeating retry while anything is pending — so a queued incident record or PAR lands promptly no matter how the failure happened, and never twice.
- **The Live Dispatch map never goes blank silently.** If the map failed to load, it used to show an empty grey pane with a green "live" badge still on top of it — claiming a status it wasn't actually showing. If the map can't load now, it falls back to the live unit list the board already has (each unit, its status, and its coordinates) under a plain "Map unavailable" heading, so the picture may be gone but the facts are still in front of the officer.
- **Inspections work with no signal.** (Prevention Core, Phase 3.6 — migration `0054`.) An inspector opens the Prevention Center once with signal and the whole day comes down to the device: the assigned and overdue inspections, the properties and their history, the code library, the checklists, and the department's own notice wording. From then on the radio can die and nothing changes. The queue still lists the day, the walkthrough still runs, violations are still cited from the code library, photographs are still taken. Nothing is written "on save": every answer and every finding is committed to the device the instant it is made, so a dead battery or a closed app loses nothing. When signal returns it all syncs by itself, and the app tells the plain truth throughout — never claiming saved-ness it does not have. **Completing an inspection works offline too**: the device records the officer's decision, and the *server* schedules the reinspection and the next cycle the moment it syncs — the software never invents a date it cannot promise, and never writes a legal record the department did not author. *(A note on an earlier version of this entry: it described the officer printing and handing over the notice in the field. That is not how inspections actually work — the physical notice is printed at the office at the end of the day and sent by certified mail. On-scene, the notice can be viewed and emailed as a courtesy copy; the served instrument is the mailed hard copy. The on-device print path was removed.)*
- **The iPad is now built for the iPad.** The inspection screen was a phone-shaped column stranded in the middle of a tablet. On a 13" iPad in landscape it now uses the full width, the checklist runs two items across so a long walk scrolls half as far, and a **findings rail is pinned alongside it** — so an officer walking a building can see everything they have cited so far without leaving the checklist, including anything flagged as an imminent hazard. It adapts down cleanly to the 11" and the mini in portrait, where the single column keeps the answer buttons big enough to hit while moving.

- **Service of notice — the notice is now provably *served*, not just printed.** (Prevention Core, Phase 3.5 — migration `0053`.) A violation notice is made valid by **service**, not by a signature: the model fire codes let you serve it personally, by mail, or by leaving it with a responsible party — and for an unattended building, by **posting it at the entrance and mailing it**. OpenFirehouse now records exactly that, as a first-class part of the record: how it was served, by which officer, to whom and in what capacity, at what address (and where that address came from), with a **photograph and GPS fix proving the posting**, and the certified-mail tracking number and its outcome. One notice can carry many service records, because the law often demands more than one method. The **Certificate of Service is generated from those records and printed on the notice itself** — the document that today is typed by hand on a court form, notarized, and scanned back in. And the **correction clock now runs from the date service was actually effected**, never from the day the notice was printed.
- **"Refused to sign" is finally a real outcome — because refusing changes nothing.** An occupant's signature only acknowledges **receipt**. It is not agreement with the findings, and refusing to sign does **not** invalidate the notice or buy more time. So the app no longer treats a missing signature as a blank space: the inspector records **Signed**, **Refused**, or **No responsible party present**. On a refusal, the app puts the three advisements on screen to read aloud — that receipt is not agreement, that the duty to correct stands regardless, and that the refusal is being recorded — and then prints exactly that on the served notice. The **inspector's own signature is now required** and can't be skipped: an unsigned notice is a defective instrument.
- **If the mail comes back, enforcement stops until you fix it.** When a certified notice is returned undelivered or unclaimed, the department *knows* the owner never got it — and the Supreme Court has held that a government that knows this "cannot simply ignore that information" and must take further reasonable steps before it takes someone's property (*Jones v. Flowers*). So the moment a returned-mail event is recorded, the inspection flips to **ACTION REQUIRED — enforcement cannot proceed**, and lists the three steps the Court actually blessed: post the premises, re-send by ordinary first-class mail, and address it to "Occupant." Posting also happens to be required outright by the property-maintenance code. The block clears only when one of those is recorded — and posting won't count without a photo and a location. No other platform we could find closes this loop; most don't record how a notice was served at all.
- **The Prevention Center: the inspector's whole day, end to end.** (Prevention Core, Phase 3 — migration `0051`.) An inspection now runs from queue to served notice without leaving the app. **My Inspections** is the working queue — what's due today, what's overdue, what's assigned to you, what nobody has picked up — searchable and filterable. **Schedule** gives the bureau a workload view (open inspections per inspector, so a pile-up is visible before it becomes one), batch scheduling across many properties at once, and bulk reassignment when staffing changes — because a staffing change shouldn't mean re-keying a month of scheduling. The **inspection runner** is the hero: a property dossier (occupancy, contacts, permits, open violations, inspection history) → a guided checklist walkthrough where answering "No" opens the citation already bound to the right code from your department's library → a findings review → **on-site signature capture** (occupant first, then inspector) → the served notice, viewable, printable, and emailable on the spot. A **violation dashboard** shows the aging picture — open violations by age bucket, the codes this district actually fails, and the repeat properties — with imminent hazards leading the list. Aging counts from the date a violation was **first reported** and never resets when it carries forward to a reinspection, so a violation cannot hide behind a fresh recheck.

- **Alert tones — dispatch, overdue units, and PAR now have a voice.** Every serious dispatch console can sound a tone; OpenFirehouse was visual-only. Now: a classic hi-lo two-tone when a call drops — **repeating until someone acknowledges the alert**, exactly like the major responder and station-alerting products (capped so an unattended display doesn't tone forever) — plus a triple beep the moment a unit crosses its overdue timer and an urgent rising pair when PAR comes due, on the dispatch console, the status board, and the station TV. Tones are **on by default** (the market norm) with a per-workstation speaker toggle to silence a machine that shouldn't sound; the overdue and PAR cues fire once per event, with the flashing UI as the persistent signal. All tones are synthesized on the spot — no audio files to fail to load offline — and opening the status board mid-shift never tones for a unit that's been overdue an hour; only new events sound. (One web-platform honesty note: browsers block all audio until the first click on a page — after any interaction, tones are live.)
- **The violation notice is now a real, printable, serveable document.** (Prevention Core, Phase 2.3 — migration `0050`.) One tap generates the notice PDF for an inspection exactly as it stands — department letterhead, premises and inspection details, every violation with its status, correct-by date, and imminent-hazard flag, the signature blocks, and the department's own legal wording. The legal text (notice body, legalese, appeal/receipt language) is **authored by the department** in Prevention Settings, matching how every major platform handles it; until it's customized, clearly-marked sample text keeps the notice presentable and reminds you to review it with your authority having jurisdiction — the software never writes legal language for you. Every generated notice is stored **inside the inspection's records** (append-only — regenerating never alters what was served before), streams on demand, and can be emailed to the owner once the platform's email key is configured (until then the notice still generates and says so plainly).
- **EMS transport statuses: Transporting and At Hospital.** Most fire departments run EMS, and the status board now speaks that language: the canonical model grows from seven to nine statuses with **Transporting** (patient aboard) and **At Hospital** (at the destination — offload, decon, restock). Both are committed statuses: not dispatchable, covered by the overdue timers (Transporting defaults to a 10-minute timer; an At-Hospital turnaround timer is available per department), included in the clear-time release prompt and orphan checks, and **never touched by a mass reset** — a rig with a patient is not a stale board entry. The cab iPad's status ladder forks at On Scene: TRANSPORTING for the ambulance, BACK IN SERVICE for the engine, then AT HOSPITAL → BACK IN SERVICE → IN QUARTERS. Every surface — status board, dispatch maps, TV display, mobile — renders the new statuses in lockstep.
- **Fixed with it: a rig already working can always walk its ladder home.** The server-side status gate shipped earlier required an active call for any committed status — which would have blocked a crew pressing ON SCENE if dispatch cleared the call early, and a transport crew arriving at the hospital after the call closed. The requirement now applies only where the doctrine aims it: *entering* the committed family (and leaving an orphaned Dispatched), never a working rig progressing or finishing.
- **The inspection workflow engine: completing an inspection now runs the full re-inspection loop, the mature-platform way.** (Prevention Core, Phase 2 — migration `0049`.) Completing an inspection is a deliberate, human-triggered act that does three things at once: stamps the completion (always the inspector's local calendar day — never a server clock), **creates the linked re-inspection carrying every open violation** (with full lineage back to the original citation — evidence photos stay with the original record), and **schedules the next cycle automatically** when the inspection type has a frequency (complete this year's annual, next year's is on the books — with a duplicate guard so a retried request can never double-book). Checklist findings (yes/no/N-A answers) are recorded with the inspection, snapshotting the question and code as answered — a later checklist edit can never rewrite what an inspector found.
- **Prevention permissions now match how real bureaus work: designated inspectors, not ranks.** Fire-prevention bureaus are often separate from suppression — inspectors are an appointed list that can include any member, any rank, even non-active personnel. Chiefs now grant and revoke **Inspector** and **Prevention Admin** designations per person (grant history is kept, never erased), and two department toggles mirror the incumbent platforms: **crew inspections** (engine companies can inspect — on by default, off for bureau-only departments) and **admin-only commit** (only prevention admins may finalize an inspection).
- **PAR checks are now a real accountability record — persisted, shared, and append-only.** The Command Board's PAR system (interval timer, countdown, PAR OVERDUE, per-person check-in) previously lived only in the command tablet's browser memory: a reload erased the PAR history, and no other screen could see the countdown. Now the PAR interval and every completed check persist to the department's records the moment they happen — who ran the check, when, how many accounted for, and each member's check-in result, in an append-only log that cannot be edited or deleted (fireground accountability history is evidence). The station TV's incident display shows the same live PAR countdown and flips to PAR OVERDUE alongside the elapsed clock, so the watch desk sees what command sees. The board UI is unchanged — it just tells the truth now.
- **The fire-prevention data model grew up: violations, code libraries, inspection types, and checklists are now first-class records.** (Prevention Core, Phase 1 — migration `0047`.) Every violation is now a queryable record of its own, carrying the full re-inspection date ladder the mature inspection platforms use (reported → scheduled recheck → actual recheck → next recheck → repaired) — powering the violation-aging dashboards and automated re-inspection scheduling coming in the next phase. Departments get an **editable violation-code library** (each entry a citation: code, short title, code body/edition/section, an optional link to the free official code viewer, and the department's own corrective wording — never copyrighted code text), **inspection types with default frequencies** (the seed for completing-an-annual-schedules-next-year's), and **checklists whose questions are pre-bound to library codes** — so citing a violation in the field will never mean hunting for a code section. One-click starter sets load the platform's standard codes and types; everything is department-scoped with database-level tenant isolation, audited, and retire-don't-delete. Existing inspection data was migrated in place with zero client changes — the API is unchanged this phase.
- **Overdue units now flag themselves — status timers with recorded status checks.** A rig sitting too long in a status with no radio traffic is the quiet failure mode of every dispatch board. Now each status carries a department-configurable timer (defaults: Dispatched 10 min — did they hear the tone?; En Route 10 min; On Scene 30 min — the crew-welfare check; off for in-quarters statuses). A unit past its threshold pulses red on the status board with its elapsed time, and dispatch gets a **Status check ✓** button: radio the rig, confirm, click — the check is recorded (who and when, append-only) and the timer restarts, exactly as the public CAD functional standard specifies. Nothing is ever changed automatically: the timer alerts a person, the person talks on the radio. Thresholds are editable in Settings → Unit Statusing (0 turns a timer off); overdue state is computed live from the status clock, so there is no scheduler to silently fail.
- **Inspection records are now legal-grade: nothing is ever silently destroyed.** (Prevention Core, Phase 0.) Deleting a property, inspection, or permit now retires the record instead of erasing it (the row is kept with a deletion timestamp — the same doctrine that already protects incident and exposure records), and every create, edit, and delete on an inspection record writes to the department's append-only audit trail, including each violation status change as who/when/from→to. Fire-inspection records surface in liens, insurance disputes, and litigation; the code now treats them that way. (Migration `0045`.)
- **Every violation now has a permanent identity, and its evidence photos are attached to *it* — not to its position in a list.** Photos were keyed to a violation's array position, so removing or reordering violations could silently re-associate photos with the wrong violation. Each violation now carries a stable id from the moment it's cited (assigned on-device in the field, so photos taken offline attach correctly before first sync); existing violations kept their historical position as their id so every already-archived photo stays exactly where it belongs.
- **When a legacy violation status is modernized, the inspector's original word is preserved.** Statuses recorded under older vocabularies ("Abated", "Pending", "UnAbated"…) display on the canonical four-state axis everywhere — but the exact word the inspector originally wrote is now kept alongside, permanently. Normalization no longer rewrites history.
- **An end-to-end regression suite now proves the inspection lifecycle over real HTTP** — legacy-status healing on read and write, audit rows for every mutation, and soft-delete retention — so none of the above can quietly regress.

### Fixed

- **Completing an inspection is now all-or-nothing.** If the reinspection step was rejected (for example, no correct-by date to schedule it from), the inspection had *already* been marked complete — leaving a finished record carrying an unabated violation with no reinspection ever scheduled, and no way to retry it. - **Un-assigned inspections are counted as unassigned.** Removing an inspector wrote a placeholder name onto the record, so the inspection dropped out of the queue's "Unassigned" count and displayed an inspector literally named "Unassigned" — two screens disagreeing about the same fact.
- **The prevention policy toggles now actually save.** "Allow crew inspections" and "Only administrators commit inspections" are permission gates, and they sat in a panel with no save button — a chief could flip one, watch it move, and leave with nothing changed. - **Legacy violation statuses now display correctly on every surface.** Records written before the canonical status axis (2026-07-11) could show as open-forever on the web and iPad while the server counted them resolved — the server now canonicalizes on read as well as write, and both clients canonicalize before comparing, so all three always agree. ### Security
- **Provisioning & onboarding events are audited.** Self-serve department signup,
  member invite acceptance, and join-code self-registration now write to the
  append-only audit log, alongside the existing coverage of member invites, rank
  verifications, and deactivations — a complete trail of who joined and when.
- **Signup verifies the department's email.** Self-serve signup now requires a
  contact email and sends a single-use verification link; the address is marked
  verified when the link is clicked. This filters bot/spam signups and confirms a
  reachable, real department — without blocking login (you can use the app while
  unverified; verification is a trust signal, not a gate).
- **Database-level department isolation is now enforced, not just app-level.**
  Previously, every department's records were kept apart only by application
  code adding a "which department" filter to each query — a single missed
  filter could have exposed one department's data to another. The application
  now connects to the database as a restricted account that is itself subject
  to per-department row security: the database independently refuses to return
  or modify any row outside the signed-in department, regardless of what the
  application asks for. This is defense-in-depth — even a bug in application
  code can no longer cross the department boundary. The login step uses a
  narrowly-scoped, audited path to look up a user's department before that
  boundary is applied. No change to what any user sees; the guarantee behind
  it is now much stronger and ready for multiple departments on one
  deployment.
- **Per-department licensing.** License activation, status, and deactivation
  are now scoped to the signed-in department and gated to chief-level users,
  so one deployment can serve many departments each with its own license.
  Activation refuses a license whose department doesn't match the caller's.
- **Closed a database privilege gap in department membership.** The restricted
  database account the application uses could, in principle, have written
  department-membership records directly. It no longer can: all membership
  changes now flow through a small set of audited, access-checked database
  routines, and those routines are executable only by the application account —
  never by any anonymous or public database role. This removes a path by which a
  signed-in user could have attached themselves to a department they do not
  belong to. (Groundwork for self-serve department signup and member onboarding.)
- **Member invites are now covered by database-level department isolation too.**
  The member-invite table was the last one still relying on application code alone
  to keep departments apart, because the "accept invite" step runs before anyone is
  signed in. Accepting an invite now goes through a single audited, access-checked
  database routine (executable only by the application account), which let us turn
  on the same per-department row security that protects every other table. Invites
  remain redeemable by the one-time link as before — the isolation guarantee behind
  them is now enforced by the database, not just the app.
- **Legal records can no longer be wiped by deleting a member.** Exposure records
  and personnel actions are legally retained, subpoenable history. They were set to
  auto-delete if a member record were ever deleted; the database now refuses to
  delete a member who still has such records attached. Member removal already works
  by deactivation (never deletion), so this adds a hard safety net beneath that rule.

### Security
- **Department data is no longer readable directly from the database.** The
  Supabase database exposes a public REST API, and with row-level security off,
  anyone holding the app's public key (which ships in the browser) could read
  member, incident, and even login tables — including hashed passwords —
  straight from the database, bypassing the server entirely. Row-level security
  is now enabled (deny-all) on all 91 remaining OpenFirehouse tables, so that
  public path returns nothing. The app is unaffected (it connects as the table
  owner, which bypasses the rule). Verified: reads that previously dumped
  password hashes now return empty.
- **CAD dispatch webhooks now require authentication.** The incoming-dispatch
  endpoints accept a call and notify every member's device — and the catch-all
  ("generic") endpoint had no authentication at all, so anyone who learned the
  URL could inject a fake dispatch. All CAD webhooks now require a shared secret
  (`CAD_WEBHOOK_SECRET`, sent as a header or URL parameter), enforced centrally
  so every vendor path is covered. In production the endpoint fails closed if no
  secret is configured, and the webhook routes are rate-limited.
- **Closed a direct-to-database read of stored integration credentials.** The
  CAD-connection and radio-config tables — which hold the API keys for the
  dispatch and radio feeds — were readable over the database's public REST API
  using the anon key that ships in the browser app, bypassing the server
  entirely. Row-level security is now enabled on both (deny-all to that public
  path); the app is unaffected (it connects as the table owner). Verified: a
  read that returned the full credential rows now returns nothing.
- **Per-department query indexes on the remaining hot tables.** Added the
  foreign-key covering indexes the database advisor flagged (17 in all,
  including the live unit-status board's apparatus join) so per-department reads
  don't full-scan as history and departments grow.
- **Role-based authority on privileged actions.** Editing the member roster now
  requires officer rank or above; department settings (FLSA/overtime config),
  the TV display PIN, and bulk data imports now require chief authority. The
  role-level map is enforced server-side and kept in lock-step with the app's
  access levels by a guard test.
- **Department isolation, line-by-line.** Every API route was read end-to-end
  and every remaining cross-department hole closed: another department can no
  longer read your training records, 911 caller details, Knox-box access
  history, or push notifications, nor modify your vacancy fills, mutual-aid
  resources, fundraising totals, or messages. Static guard tests now catch
  four additional bug patterns so these can't regress silently.
- **Uploaded files are department-private.** Fireground photos and
  correspondence attachments are now served only to members of the owning
  department (previously any signed-in user who knew a filename could fetch
  them on self-hosted installs).
- **Attachment links are validated.** Attachment URLs must be real web links
  or in-app paths — script-injection style links are rejected.
- **API keys can no longer leak into logs or error messages.** AI provider
  errors (which can echo your API key) are redacted everywhere they're
  logged or returned.
- **Stricter input validation on public endpoints** (login, TV display,
  hazmat lookups, CAD webhooks) with clear validation errors.
- **Prompt-injection guards on all AI actions.** Text inside your records

### Fixed
- **Incident timeline "replay" could leak a timer.** Replaying an incident
  timeline started a 400ms interval with no cleanup if you navigated away
  mid-replay (or clicked Replay again), leaving a stray timer ticking state on
  an unmounted view. It's now stored in a ref, cleared on unmount, and a
  re-click cancels the prior run first. (Found in an app-wide reliability sweep;
  every other timer, socket, and realtime channel was already cleaned up
  correctly, and all socket reconnects already use capped backoff.)
- **Dispatch notification could crash the app on a real call.** The dispatch
  toast assumed the units field was an array and called `.join()` on it, but CAD
  alerts store units as a comma-separated string — so the moment a real dispatch
  populated the toast it threw `units.join is not a function` and tripped the
  "Something went wrong" screen. The toast now normalizes units the same way the
  dispatch feed does. (Latent since the SSE stream rarely delivered on Vercel;
  surfaced once real-time delivery became reliable.)

### Added
- **Auto-status from CAD.** When a dispatch arrives, the apparatus named in the
  call are automatically set to **Dispatched**, so the unit-status board and TV
  light up the instant a call drops — no manual taps. Matching handles both full
  names and the abbreviations real CAD feeds use (`E6`→Engine 6, `BC`→Battalion 1,
  `B14`→Brush 14, etc.), and is **fail-safe**: an abbreviation is only accepted
  when it resolves to exactly one apparatus in your fleet — ambiguous (e.g. `T1`
  when you run both a Truck 1 and a Tanker 1) or unknown units are skipped, never
  guessed. Best-effort; never blocks the CAD webhook response.
- **Unit-status board on the Dispatch & Command page** — the dispatcher now sees
  the incoming-call feed and live apparatus status together in one view.
- **Live per-unit status lifecycle (Phase 2).** Every apparatus now has a live,

  Staging → Committed → Returning — on a new **Unit Status — Live** board on the
  Dashboard. New `unit_statuses` + `unit_status_history` tables (separate from
  the maintenance `apparatus.status`), `GET /api/units/status`,
  `PATCH /api/units/:id/status`, `POST /api/units/reset`. Updates show live via
  **Supabase Realtime Broadcast** — the server pushes a per-station "changed"
  signal on every change and the client re-fetches the authoritative API (SSE
  does not work on Vercel serverless; Broadcast exposes no table data and needs
  no RLS change), with a 30s poll as backstop. Per-unit status-change history is

  optimistic with a server-authoritative reconcile.

### Security
- **Clearing/closing a call is now dispatch-controlled.** `DELETE /api/active-board`
  (close the incident) requires Dispatch or a Chief (new shared
  `middleware/requireDispatch.js`; was open to any authenticated user — a
  firefighter could close the station's incident). Changing a unit's status
  requires Dispatch/command (any unit) or the rig's officer for their own unit
  (incl. acting A/C, A/LT, resolved from today's run list; fail-closed). The CAD

### Fixed
- Dashboard was not receiving the `user` prop, which silently disabled every
  role-gated control on it (the new Clear Call button and unit-status edit
  controls). Now passed through.
- **TV display 24/7 stability.** The radio ticker's WebSocket targeted an
  endpoint that can't exist on serverless and reconnected every 5 seconds
  indefinitely — on an always-on wall display that churned thousands of dead
  connections a day. Now uses capped exponential backoff and tears down
  cleanly (it already falls back to the polled radio feed). Also removed a
  realtime-client resource leak and added a 6-hour idle reload so a TV left up
  for days flushes accumulated browser state — never during an active incident.
- **App-wide reconnect hardening.** An audit of every persistent connection
  found four reconnect loops using a fixed 5-second retry (the two `/api/cad/stream`
  SSE connections — one of which runs on every page for a logged-in user — and
  the radio-feed WebSockets). On serverless these hammered unreachable endpoints
  thousands of times a day on always-on screens. All now use one shared
  capped-exponential-backoff helper (5s → 5min, reset on a healthy connection).
  Also audited: no leaked interval timers or event listeners; connection feeds
  are length-capped.
